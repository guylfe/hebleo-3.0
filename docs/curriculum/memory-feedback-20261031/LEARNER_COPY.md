## Page 4: How memory works

[Stable page: MEMORY-A2.learn.connection]

Let’s give the same digits some meaning.

[Number transformation; reveal dates/events only after first attempt or skip]

`0704177609112001` → `07041776 · 09112001` → `07/04/1776 · 09/11/2001`

American month–day–year order:

- July 4, 1776 — the American Declaration of Independence.
- September 11, 2001 — the September 11 attacks.

Same digits, different meaning. Three ideas help:

1. **Chunking:** Think of two meaningful dates instead of sixteen separate digits.

2. **Logical constraints:** A date limits the possibilities: months run from 01 to 12, and a day cannot be 47.

3. **Memory hooks:** Connect new information to established old knowledge. If you know these events and their dates, they give the digits something familiar to connect to. If not, grouping and date structure can still help.

[Conditional comparison feedback, unchanged from source]

- exact: “That’s the original sequence. Notice which part of the connection helped you bring it back.”
- reverse: “You recalled both dates, but swapped their order. The original starts with July 4, 1776, then September 11, 2001.”
- zeros: “You recalled the dates. In the original display, months and days use two digits: 07/04 and 09/11. Those zeroes are part of the original sequence.”
- dayMonth: “You used day–month–year. These are the same dates; the demonstration uses month–day–year, so its sequence begins 07/04/1776.”
- partial: “Here’s the original number alongside your attempt. Compare the two date groups and notice which parts came back to you.”
- empty: “Here are the two dates within the original number. Notice how the grouping, format and familiar events can help you make sense of it.”
