OUTCOME: Experience the contrast between an unstructured number and meaningful dates; distinguish chunking, logical constraints and connections to established knowledge.
CONNECT TO: Familiar dates illustrate three useful principles. All three will return with Hebrew roots and patterns; the immediate focus is connecting new information to established old information.
PREREQUISITES: None.

Screen 4: Give the number meaning · MEMORY-A2.learn.connection
LEARNER TEXT:
That was quite a lot of digits to remember! Now watch what happens when we organise the same number differently.
[PLACEHOLDER / AUTHOR INSTRUCTION] ANIMATION MEMORY-NUMBER-TO-DATES: on Show the connection, start with 0704177609112001. Keep all sixteen digits in the same order and separate them into 07041776 · 09112001. Then insert slashes to produce 07/04/1776 · 09/11/2001. Only spacing and separators change; no digits appear, disappear or swap places. Reveal the event labels after the transformation. Reduced-motion and nonvisual alternatives present the same three stages with a Next step control and a short spoken/text description. Replaying this explanatory animation is allowed; it does not restart the recall task.
07/04/1776 · 09/11/2001
These dates use American month–day–year order: July 4, 1776, and September 11, 2001.
The first is the date of the American Declaration of Independence; the second is the date of the September 11 attacks. We haven’t changed any of the digits. We’ve changed how we understand them, using three different principles.
Chunking: instead of trying to hold sixteen separate digits in mind, we can think of two meaningful units—the two dates. Each date still contains several digits, but we can treat it as one item.
Logical constraints: a date has a structure, and that structure limits the possibilities. A month must be between 01 and 12, so 19 cannot be the month. A day cannot be 47. Knowing the format helps us rule out possibilities instead of treating every digit as completely open.
Connecting new information to established old information: if you already know these events and their dates, the number connects to knowledge you already have. If the events are unfamiliar, this particular connection will be less useful to you, even though the grouping and date structure may still help.

Screen 5: What made the difference? · MEMORY-A2.learn.meaning
LEARNER TEXT:
While we’ll use all 3 principles to enhance our learning, right now I want to focus on the third one: connect new information to established old information. You already have a great deal of knowledge to build on from your life experience. A useful connection gives the new information a place in that knowledge. At first we’ll connect brand new Hebrew concepts to things you’re already familiar with from English, and as we move forward we’ll connect new Hebrew concepts to older Hebrew concepts you’re already comfortable with.
All three principles will come back when we explore Hebrew roots and patterns. For now, let’s make sure we can tell what each one contributed to this example.
[PLACEHOLDER / AUTHOR INSTRUCTION] OPTIONAL SLIDE-DOWN MEMORY-ASSOCIATIVE-NETWORK: collapsed by default, labelled “How does the associative network memory model explain this?” Open and close on request; opening it is not required for progress and adds no quiz. Keep focus on the control, expose its expanded/collapsed state, and respect reduced motion.
OPTIONAL LEARNER TEXT: One useful way to think about memory is as a network of connected ideas. Bringing one idea to mind can make related ideas easier to reach. For example, thinking about American Independence Day may bring July 4 to mind, which then helps you recover part of the number. In an associative network model, those links help explain how one idea can lead to another. We’ll use connections like these to give new Hebrew material a familiar starting point.
MEMORY-A2.embedded.what-changed — matching
LEARNER PROMPT: Match each observation to the principle that explains it.
OBSERVATIONS:
1. Thinking of two dates instead of sixteen separate digits.
2. Ruling out 19 as the month because a year has only twelve months.
3. Using your existing knowledge of American Independence Day to recover July 4.
RESPONSE OPTIONS (each used once):
A. Logical constraints
B. Connecting new information to established old information
C. Chunking
ANSWER / ACCEPTED MODEL: 1 → C (Chunking); 2 → A (Logical constraints); 3 → B (Connecting new information to established old information).
FEEDBACK for observation 1: “Chunking. You’re grouping the digits into two larger units.”
FEEDBACK for observation 2: “Logical constraints. Knowing how months work rules out an impossible value.”
FEEDBACK for observation 3: “Connecting new information to established old information. Something you already know helps you recover the date.”
