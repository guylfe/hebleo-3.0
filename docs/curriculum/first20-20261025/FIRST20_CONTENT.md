## 1. Give new information a familiar home — MEMORY

Legacy alias: A001. Connection: Start with something you already know.

Source: [original presentation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ImMGqVDWT8BMN63xay1-DT7ug3Z1W9mr/view), slides 7, 8, 9.

Source: [original lesson 25301](https://hebleo.com/courses/hebleo-hebrew-learning-platform/lessons/introduction-welcome-and-outline/).

Adaptation: The source number and date association are retained. No speed-of-learning claim is repeated. Interactive recall, replay and accessibility controls are adaptations.

### Page 1: Learn — A small experiment

We will use connections to make new information easier to remember. First, try a short recall task. There is nothing to study yet.

On the next page, press Play when you are ready. A number will appear for ten seconds and then disappear. Try to remember it without copying it.

### Page 2: Learn — Ten seconds to remember

On Play, show **0704177609112001** for **10 seconds**, then remove it and request recall. No autoplay; replay/reset and untimed alternative are provided.

When the number disappears, type what you remember. Leading zeroes matter.

### Page 3: Learn — Give the digits meaning

The source demonstration connects the number to two dates: 04 July 1776 and 11 September 2001.

07041776 · 09112001

The digits use month–day–year order: 07/04/1776 and 09/11/2001. If the events are familiar to you, they provide two anchors for an otherwise long string. If they are unfamiliar, the association will be less useful to you.

The point we will use throughout this course: connect new information to established old information.

### Page 4: Check — What made a useful connection?

Which explanation fits this demonstration?

1. A familiar meaning can help organise new information.
2. A meaningful connection guarantees perfect recall.
3. The digits changed between attempts.

After Check: **A familiar meaning can help organise new information.**. The number stayed the same. A familiar association can provide a way to organise and retrieve it; your own recall may vary.

### Page 5: Learn — Use the idea with letters

Next, we will connect Hebrew letter shapes to familiar images. Choose an association that helps you recognise the printed letter. Then try to recall it before looking at the explanation again.

For a personal practice round, think of three familiar objects. Look away, name them, do a different short task, and try once more.

### Page 6: Continue — Keep the connection

Start with something you already know.

Try one example again without looking. Then continue to a different topic; this material will return later.

## 2. Your first letters: מ נ ת — MNT

Legacy alias: A002. Connection: Use the familiar-image strategy from MEMORY.

Adaptation: Source mnemonics retained as text. Original mnemonic drawings/audio are not fabricated. Recognition replaces tracing, following Guy’s decision.

### Page 1: Learn — Three shapes, three sounds

מ — Mem: /m/, as in mountain.

נ — Nun: /n/, as in nose.

ת — Tav: /t/, as in tripping.

Focus first on the printed shape and its sound. The letter names will become familiar through use.

### Page 2: Learn — Attach each shape to an image

Guy’s source mnemonics are mountain-climber for מ, nose for נ, and tripping for ת.

Look at one letter, say its sound, then look away and picture it. Use the association to recover the shape.

### Page 3: Learn — Two letters have an end form

מ → ם — Mem at the end of a word uses ם.

נ → ן — Nun at the end of a word uses ן.

The final form changes the shape, not the identity of the letter. Tav keeps the same shape at the end.

### Page 5: Check — Recognise a final form

1. ן
2. ת
3. ם

After Check: **ם**. מ and ם are two forms of the same letter.

## 3. Read from right to left — WRITING-BASICS

Adaptation: Only printed forms are required. Handwriting remains optional off-screen practice; no tracing or simulated script glyphs.

### Page 1: Learn — Find the beginning

Hebrew words run from right to left. In the printed sequence below, start with the rightmost letter.

מנת

The reading order is Mem, then Nun, then Tav. We are identifying letters here, not guessing the word’s vowels.

### Page 2: Learn — Keep the right shape at the end

The end of a Hebrew word is on its left. A final Mem or Nun takes its final shape there.

תן — Tav, then final Nun: the leftmost letter ends the string.

תם — Tav, then final Mem.

These are letter-order exercises at this stage. Their meanings are not new vocabulary targets.

### Page 3: Learn — Print and handwriting

The course uses printed, or block, letters. Hebrew also has handwritten forms, often called script. These can look different while representing the same letters.

For now, recognise and type the printed forms. Optional handwriting practice belongs in a separate printable resource.

## 4. Add a, e and i — VOWELS-AEI

Legacy alias: A004. Connection: The letters give you consonants; marks can supply a vowel.

Source: [original presentation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ImMGqVDWT8BMN63xay1-DT7ug3Z1W9mr/view), slides 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28, 80.

Adaptation: The source’s blanket horizontal-line rule is narrowed to the displayed patah/qamats examples. Hataf-qamats is not taught as /a/. Source nonsense strings are identified as decoding practice. Silent sheva is introduced earlier for the revised route.

### Page 1: Learn — Letters and vowel marks

Compare the same Mem with three different marks. Read the letter and then its vowel.

מַ — ma — the line supplies /a/.

מֶ — me — the dots supply /e/.

מִ — mi — the single dot supplies /i/.

The vowel marks are called Nikkud. We will use them to make unfamiliar readings explicit.

### Page 2: Learn — Recognise the familiar patterns

The displayed מַ and מָ examples are read with /a/. Guy’s mnemonic is a horizontal line for the open “aaaa” sound.

מֶ and מֵ show /e/. The pair of dots is like elephant eyes. מִ has one itsy-bitsy dot for /i/.

Learn the reading of the supplied examples. These shortcuts do not mean every superficially similar mark always has the same sound.

### Page 3: Learn — A stop between sounds

A silent sheva means no vowel follows that consonant in the supplied reading.

Inspect the mark under תְ: in the silent-sheva model, the Tav has no extra vowel after it. This is a letter-and-mark exercise. Sheva has other uses that return later.

### Page 5: Check — Change just the vowel

## 6. Point to the people in a conversation — PRONOUNS

Legacy alias: A006. Connection: You can now read the letters in these pronouns.

Source: [original lesson 32299](https://hebleo.com/courses/hebleo-hebrew-learning-platform/lessons/text-lesson-pronouns-and-plurals/).

Adaptation: Human-written source retained in shortened form. He and we remain at their later readable stages. Social judgments about everyday variants are omitted.

### Page 1: Learn — Speaker, listener, someone else

A pronoun points to a person or group already clear in the conversation.

I is the speaker. You is the person being addressed. She and they refer to people being talked about, who may also be present.

אני — I — used by a male or female speaker.

### Page 2: Learn — Four ways to address you

אתה — you — one man or boy

את — you — one woman or girl

אתם — you — a male or mixed group

אתן — you — an all-female group

The shared opening את helps connect the four forms. Choose by who is being addressed.

### Page 3: Learn — Talk about someone

היא — she

הם — they — a male or mixed group

הן — they — an all-female group

These are subject pronouns. Object forms such as “me” and “her” come later. “He” and “we” also arrive after their remaining letters.

אני אמן. — I am an artist — a male speaker in this example.

היי is the supplied spelling of the greeting. Keep its doubled Yod as part of the word; the spelling return will make another connection later.
