# Hebleo pedagogy: evidence-based best practices

Status: **research synthesis and proposals. Not approved curriculum.** Prepared 2026-10-04 by the Hebleo Curriculum Designer for Guy Landau.

This document deliberately ignores how the app is built. It brings together:

1. Guy's four earlier research documents:
   - *Online learning summary*
   - *Grammar / Ulpan level summary*
   - *Paul Nation summary*
   - *Deep research report* (translation, the audio→spelling ladder, skip-resistance)
2. A new review of the research literature on memory, instructed second-language acquisition (ISLA), vocabulary, feedback, pronunciation, reading and script, Hebrew orthography and morphology, multimedia design, and online and self-paced learning.

Guy's teaching decisions and linguistic judgment remain canonical. Nothing here changes a lesson.

- Hebrew examples are **illustrative proposed wording** for discussion. Each needs Guy's linguistic review before any use.
- Every recommendation is labelled:
  - **Preserve**: keep it; the evidence supports it.
  - **Improve**: keep the idea, change how it is done.
  - **Reconsider**: open a decision for Guy.

## How strong is the evidence?

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **A** | Several meta-analyses or a large, consistent body of experiments |
| **B** | One meta-analysis, or several good experiments, with known caveats |
| **C** | One or two studies, or a well-supported theory not yet tested in this setting |
| **D** | Design judgment: an inference from A–C applied to Hebleo |

Effect sizes (d / g) are given where a meta-analysis reports them. As a rough guide, 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large. Results from different meta-analyses are not directly comparable.

## 1. The core principle: connect new information to established old information

Guy's principle is well supported, with one important refinement: **the connection has to be made, not assumed.**

What the evidence says:

- **Prior knowledge speeds up learning when the new material fits it.** In the SLIMM model, information that fits an existing schema is consolidated faster and with less effort. Information that does not fit gets a separate "novelty" boost (van Kesteren et al., 2012). **Grade B.**
- **Having prior knowledge does not by itself predict gains.** Across roughly 500 studies, the average correlation between prior knowledge and later knowledge gain was about zero (r ≈ −.06), with very large variation (Simonsmeier et al., 2022). Prior knowledge helps when the lesson *activates* it and links the new item to it. **Grade A** for "not automatic"; **Grade D** for the design inference.
- **Related items learned at the same time interfere with each other.** Words introduced as a semantic set (colours, family members) cause more mix-up errors, especially when practised massed. Spacing reduces the interference (Nakata & Suzuki, 2019, SSLA). A 2026 multilevel meta-analysis of semantic clustering exists; only its abstract was accessible. **Grade B.**
- **Guessing before being taught helps only when the guess can draw on existing knowledge.** Failed retrieval attempts improve later learning (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009). The benefit appears when the guess is meaningfully related to the answer, not for arbitrary pairs (Huelser & Metcalfe, 2012). Errorful generation improves memory for items more than for arbitrary associations. **Grade B.**
- **Hebrew roots are a powerful anchor, but L2 learners don't use them automatically.** A masked-priming study found that advanced L2 Hebrew speakers extract roots more weakly and shallowly than native speakers. Root and pattern awareness has to be taught explicitly. **Grade C.**

What this means for Hebleo:

**1.1 Preserve: connect-new-to-old as the organising rule of the whole course. (Grade B/D)**

**1.2 Improve: make every connection explicit and active. (Grade D)**

Each new item names its anchor and makes the learner use it:

- "You already know X; here is Y."
- A retrieval of X immediately before Y is presented.
- Optionally, a guess at Y from X.

> *Illustrative, proposed — needs Guy's linguistic review:* learners already know the infinitive **לִכְתּוֹב**. The future is introduced as "swap the **ל** for a person prefix": **אֶכְתּוֹב / תִּכְתּוֹב / יִכְתּוֹב**. This also works for Pi'el (**לְדַבֵּר → אֲדַבֵּר**) and, with the **ה** dropping, for Hif'il and Hitpa'el (**לְהַזְמִין → אַזְמִין**, **לְהִתְלַבֵּשׁ → אֶתְלַבֵּשׁ**). Verbs where the vowel changes (e.g. **לִלְמוֹד → אֶלְמַד**, **לִקְנוֹת → אֶקְנֶה**) must be taught openly as exceptions to the anchor, not hidden.

**1.3 Improve: anchor to established knowledge, not to something taught the same day. (Grade B)**

- A new item should lean on something the learner has already retrieved successfully across sessions.
- Look-alikes introduced together (two binyanim, minimal-pair words, similar suffixes) compete with each other.

**1.4 Improve: stagger confusable items, then contrast them. (Grade B)**

1. Introduce A.
2. Let A settle over at least one later session.
3. Introduce B, anchored to A ("unlike A…").
4. Only then mix A and B in practice (§4).

**1.5 Improve: use "guess first" only where a guess is possible. (Grade B)**

- Good: guessing a word from a known root, or a form from a known pattern.
- Not useful: blind guessing of an arbitrary new word.

## 2. Lesson shape: Learn / Check / Vocabulary / Apply / Continue

Guy's earlier documents describe the cycle *explain → examples → controlled practice → meaningful production → delayed retrieval*, along with *recognition → recall → production*. Hebleo's five-part lesson already follows this. The research mostly suggests changing what happens *inside* each part.

| Part | Job in the research cycle | Main evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Learn** | Short explicit explanation plus worked examples, anchored to old knowledge | Explicit instruction beats implicit for simple and complex features: d = 0.73 and 0.88 (Spada & Tomita, 2010). Segmenting and pretraining reduce load (Mayer). **A** |
| **Check** | Notice and understand the form before producing it (recognition) | Input-based (processing) instruction is strongest on comprehension tests; output practice matches it on production (Shintani, 2015). **B** |
| **Vocabulary** | Retrieval practice on the lesson's words in both directions | Flashcard-style retrieval gives large gains (Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara, 2020). Productive (L1→L2) retrieval builds both kinds of knowledge (Nakata). **A** |
| **Apply** | Sentence-centred production, from controlled to meaningful, with informative feedback | Skill acquisition theory: practice must resemble the target use (DeKeyser). Prompts beat recasts (Lyster & Saito, 2010). **A/B** |
| **Continue** | Delayed retrieval of earlier material: the spiral | Spacing g = 0.76 in L2 learning (Kim & Webb, 2022). Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky). **A** |

**2.1 Preserve: the five-part concise lesson. (Grade A/D)**

- Short, segmented lessons match the segmenting and pretraining principles (Mayer, d ≈ 0.4–0.8 across meta-analyses).
- The fixed sequence lowers cognitive load: learners don't have to work out each lesson's format.

**2.2 Improve: Learn = one idea, one anchor, two or three worked examples, then stop. (Grade A/D)**

- Explanation is minimal and in English.
- Examples are full sentences with native audio.
- The section ends as soon as the learner can see the rule. No grammar essays.

**2.3 Improve: Check should require attending to the form in order to get the meaning. (Grade B)**

- Example: hear **אֶכְתּוֹב** vs **כָּתַבְתִּי** and pick *will* or *did*.
- An item the learner can pass by ignoring the target form is not a check.

**2.4 Improve: Vocabulary moves through recognition → recall → production, in both directions. (Grade A)**

**2.5 Improve: Apply is where most of the time goes. (Grade A/D)**

- Every item is a sentence.
- Every item has one explicit target, as Källkvist's translation work requires.
- Every item combines exactly one new element with established ones (§7).

**2.6 Improve: Continue is a real, scheduled retrieval of older material, not a "next lesson" button. (Grade A)**

- It is the main way spiral reuse happens.
- See §3.

## 3. Spacing, retrieval and spiral reuse

What the evidence says:

- **Spacing works.** In L2 learning specifically, spaced practice beats massed practice (g = 0.76, 48 experiments; Kim & Webb, 2022). **Grade A.**
- **The best gap depends on how long the knowledge must last.** Very short and very long gaps are both worse than intermediate ones. As the retention target grows, the best gap shrinks as a proportion of it: roughly 20–40% of a one-week horizon, down to about 5–10% of a one-year horizon (Cepeda et al., 2008). **Grade A.**
- **Expanding gaps help a little; the total amount of spacing matters more** (Nakata, 2015). **Grade B.**
- **Smaller study sets don't help on their own.** What matters is the spacing between retrievals (Nakata & Webb, 2016). **Grade B.**
- **Successive relearning is the strongest combination.** Retrieve until correct within a session, then repeat across several later sessions. In course settings this raised exam performance (about 84% vs 72%; Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli, 2013). **Grade A/B.**
- **Retrieval beats re-study, and learners consistently misjudge this.** They prefer massed practice, blocked practice and re-reading because those *feel* more fluent (Bjork's "desirable difficulties"). **Grade A.**
- **Adaptive scheduling works at scale.** Duolingo's half-life regression model raised daily engagement by about 12% (Settles & Meeder, 2016). FSRS-type schedulers report about 20–30% fewer reviews than SM-2 at equal retention; that figure is community and industry benchmarking, not peer-reviewed. **Grade B/C.**

What this means for Hebleo:

**3.1 Preserve: spiral reuse as a core design feature. (Grade A)**

**3.2 Improve: plan the spiral at three timescales. (Grade A/D)**

- **Within a lesson:** a new item comes back later in the same lesson after other items (Learn → … → Apply).
- **Across lessons (curriculum spiral):** each new item is deliberately reused in the next one or two lessons, roughly a week later, and again in a later unit, ideally inside new sentences combined with newer material.
- **Personal review:** an adaptive layer schedules individual words and sentences by each learner's own forgetting. This is a design principle only; how to build it is out of scope here.

**3.3 Improve: use successive relearning for core items. (Grade A/B)**

- Practise each core item until it is retrieved correctly once.
- Bring it back on at least two or three later days.

**3.4 Improve: measure learning by delayed retrieval, not by immediate success. (Grade A)**

- Immediate success during a lesson overstates learning.
- Mastery should mean "retrieved correctly after a gap".

**3.5 Reconsider: is the spiral authored, scheduled, or both? (Grade D)**

- The research supports both: authored recycling in sentences, plus personal scheduling.
- Guy should decide how much of the spiral is fixed by lesson design and how much is left to a scheduler. See §15.

## 4. Blocking vs interleaving (especially for binyanim and look-alike forms)

What the evidence says:

- **Mixing grammar structures in practice helps retention.** Interleaved grammar practice beat blocked practice at a one-week delay, even though learners made more errors during practice (Nakata & Suzuki, 2019, MLJ). **Grade B.**
- **Interleaving helps most when the categories are hard to tell apart; it is weak or negative for word lists and expository text.** Overall g = 0.42 (Brunmair & Richter, 2019). **Grade A.**
- **Learning similar words together causes interference, which spacing reduces** (§1). **Grade B.**

**4.1 Improve: block first, then interleave. (Grade B/D)**

- Teach and practise a new structure on its own (blocked) until it is understood.
- Once it is established, mix it with the structures it gets confused with.
- Example: Pa'al vs Pi'el future, past vs future, ל״ה vs regular verbs.

**4.2 Improve: interleave to train discrimination, not as a general habit. (Grade A)**

- The value comes from having to decide *which* form applies.
- Mixing unrelated items helps little.
- Interleaving word lists may be counter-productive.

**4.3 Preserve: "repeat the language, vary the task" (Nation). (Grade A/D)**

- It fits the evidence on varied retrieval and on spacing.

## 5. Explicit grammar, input and output

What the evidence says:

- **Explicit instruction is effective for both simple and complex features:** d = 0.73 and 0.88 (Spada & Tomita, 2010). **Grade A.**
- **Explicit and implicit instruction both give large immediate effects; implicit effects may last somewhat longer** (Kang, Sok & Han, 2019, 54 studies). **Grade A.**
- **Input-based practice wins on comprehension tests.** Output-based practice matches it on production and beats it when both get the same explicit information (Shintani, 2015). **Grade B.**
- **Skills move from declarative to procedural to automatic knowledge.** Automatisation needs a lot of practice that resembles real use: meaningful, under some time pressure, transfer-appropriate (DeKeyser; Suzuki & DeKeyser). **Grade A/B.**

**5.1 Preserve: short explicit grammar in Learn. (Grade A)**

**5.2 Improve: put comprehension before production for each new structure. (Grade B)**

- Check (input) comes before Apply (output).
- Both are needed. Neither replaces the other.

**5.3 Improve: build in a fluency strand. (Grade A/B)**

- Guy's earlier research found Hebleo strong at understanding (stages 1–3) and weak at automaticity (stages 4–7). The evidence agrees this is the main gap.
- Proposal: easy, already-known material practised faster. Examples:
  - timed recognition
  - quick-fire sentence building from known words
  - listening at natural speed
  - repeat-and-shorten speaking (§9)
- This is Nation's fourth strand.

**5.4 Reconsider: grammar scope at the Bet/Gimel boundary. (Grade D, needs Guy)**

- Guy's grammar mapping identified systematic future tense as the main gap.
- Items still to verify:
  - infinitive constructions
  - copula in past and future
  - יש / אין / היה / יהיה
  - negative imperatives with **אַל** + future
  - comparatives and superlatives
  - connectors of cause, purpose and time
- This is a curriculum decision and stays open.

## 6. Corrective feedback

What the evidence says:

- **Corrective feedback works and its effect lasts:** d ≈ 0.61–0.64, maintained on delayed tests (Li, 2010). **Grade A.**
- **Prompts beat recasts.** Making the learner self-correct is stronger than simply supplying the correct form, especially on free-production measures (Lyster & Saito, 2010). **Grade A.**
- **In computer-based learning, elaborated feedback is far stronger than a bare correct/incorrect signal:** g = 0.56 overall; elaborated 0.49 vs knowledge-of-correct-result 0.05 (TESL-EJ, 2020). Immediate feedback suits lower-level outcomes; in L2 spacing studies it was very large (g = 1.04; Kim & Webb, 2022). **Grade A/B.**
- **Errors followed by correction are valuable.** Confident errors that get corrected are remembered especially well (Metcalfe). **Grade B.**

**6.1 Preserve: immediate feedback on every response. (Grade A)**

**6.2 Improve: never just "Incorrect". (Grade A)**

Feedback should say *what* is wrong in terms of the lesson's target. For example:

- "Future of **לִכְתּוֹב** for *I*: the prefix is **אֶ־**"
- "This is the past form"

**6.3 Improve: on the first error, prompt; on the second, show the answer. (Grade A/B)**

- First error: a hint, a pointer to the anchor, or a chance to retry.
- Second error: the answer, with the reason.
- Re-queue the item later in the session (successive relearning).

**6.4 Improve: anticipate common errors and author targeted feedback for them. (Grade D)**

- Wrong binyan prefix, wrong gender, missing **אֶת**, and so on.
- This is the highest-value authoring effort per minute.

## 7. Sentences, chunks and the "halo"

What the evidence says:

- **Paying attention to formulaic sequences improves perceived fluency** (Boers et al., 2006; Boers & Lindstromberg, 2012). **Grade B.**
- **For input, about 98% known words is needed for comfortable comprehension; a little unknown language around a task is fine if the task doesn't depend on it** (Nation, from Guy's summary). **Grade A/B.**

**7.1 Preserve: sentence-centred application. (Grade A/D)**

- Sentences carry grammar, vocabulary, chunks and pronunciation together.
- They make old items reappear naturally.

**7.2 Improve: "one new thing per sentence". (Grade D, from A/B evidence)**

- Each Apply sentence contains one target and otherwise only established material.
- The rest of the sentence *is* the old knowledge the new item connects to.

**7.3 Improve: tag and teach high-value chunks as units. (Grade B)**

- Examples: **מָה שְׁלוֹמְךָ**, **אֵין לִי**, **בְּסֵדֶר גָּמוּר**.
- Teach them as whole chunks first; analyse them later in the spiral when the grammar arrives. This is connect-new-to-old in reverse: the chunk is the old knowledge the grammar later explains.

**7.4 Preserve: the halo principle. (Grade B)**

- Glossed unknown words are acceptable when the task does not test them.

## 8. Translation and the learner's first language

Guy's earlier research recommends keeping translation as one contextualised exercise family, about 20–30% of items. The evidence behind it:

- Contrastive explanation plus translation improved vocabulary (Laufer & Girsai, 2008).
- Translation is a legitimate teaching tool (Hall & Cook, 2012).
- Translation shifts attention towards vocabulary, so each translation item needs a clear target (Källkvist).
- L1 glosses beat L2 glosses at every proficiency level (Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara, 2020).

**8.1 Preserve: translation as one family, contextualised, with an explicit target per item. (Grade B)**

**8.2 Preserve: English glosses and English explanations. (Grade B)**

**8.3 Improve: use contrast with English deliberately as an anchor. (Grade B/D)**

- "In English you say X; Hebrew does Y."
- This is the most widely shared "old knowledge" learners have.
- Example: **יֵשׁ לִי** vs *I have*.

## 9. Listening, native audio, pronunciation and speaking

What the evidence says:

- **Same-language captions strongly help listening comprehension (g ≈ 0.99) and vocabulary** (Montero Perez et al., 2013). **Grade A.**
- **Pronunciation instruction works:** d ≈ 0.80–0.89 across 86 studies. It works better with feedback, longer interventions and controlled measures (Lee, Jang & Plonsky, 2015). **Grade A.**
- **High-variability phonetic training is very reliable.** In 97% of 32 studies, learners improved after hearing multiple talkers (around six) in varied phonetic contexts, with feedback (Thomson, 2018). **Grade A.**
- **Shadowing helps prosody, comprehensibility and bottom-up listening; evidence for individual sounds is inconclusive.** **Grade B.**
- **Speech-recognition feedback helps pronunciation:** g = 0.69; explicit feedback 0.86; individual sounds 0.82 vs prosody 0.37. It needs more than four weeks of use (Ngo, Chen & Lai, 2023). **Grade B.**
- **Repeating a spoken task with less time each round builds fluency that lasts and transfers** (4/3/2 technique; de Jong & Perfetti, 2011). **Grade B.**

**9.1 Preserve: native audio on every Hebrew sentence and word. (Grade A)**

**9.2 Improve: use several native voices for the same material. (Grade A)**

- High-variability training shows that multiple talkers build sturdier perception.
- A single voice is the weakest option.
- This affects human recording, so it is a resourcing decision for Guy.

**9.3 Improve: listening with Hebrew captions, captions fading over time. (Grade A/D)**

- Step 1: Hebrew captions on.
- Step 2: on demand.
- Step 3: off.

**9.4 Improve: a light speaking strand. (Grade B/D)**

- Listen and repeat, shadowing of known sentences, and repeat-with-less-time.
- Optional speech-recognition feedback later.
- Do not overclaim what speech recognition can judge in Hebrew; individual-sound feedback is where it is strongest.

**9.5 Preserve: the audio → spelling ladder from Guy's earlier research. (Grade B/D)**

- choose → match → arrange → type → type + say
- It is a recognition → recall → production sequence for the written form.

## 10. Script, Nikkud and reading

What the evidence says:

- **Handwriting letters speeds up script learning in adults.** Adults learning Arabic letters by handwriting reached criterion in about 2 sessions vs about 6 for typing or video, and generalised better to spelling and to reading new words (Wiley & Rapp, 2021). **Grade C**: one strong study.
- **Pointed text (with vowel diacritics) supports decoding and comprehension for beginners.** Native Hebrew readers move to unpointed text around grades 3–4, relying on root and pattern knowledge to supply the missing vowels. A study of Arabic and Hebrew readers found vowels aided comprehension. **Grade B.**
- **Extensive and graded reading works:** d ≈ 0.57 between groups and 0.79 pre–post. Effects are larger with web-based stories and when reading is built into the curriculum (Jeon & Day, 2016). **Grade A/B.**

**10.1 Preserve: curated Nikkud. (Grade B)**

**10.2 Improve: fade Nikkud as pattern knowledge grows, as native readers do. (Grade B/D)**

Proposed stages:

1. Full pointing on all new material.
2. Pointing only on new or ambiguous words within sentences.
3. Unpointed text, with pointing available on demand.

Two cautions:

- The fade should follow mastered patterns, not lesson numbers.
- How the stages are set is Guy's decision.

**10.3 Reconsider: optional handwriting practice for the alphabet stage. (Grade C)**

- Paper is fine: "copy these five letters".
- The evidence is one strong study; low cost if offered as optional.

**10.4 Improve: add short graded readings built only from known material. (Grade A/B)**

- Each reading recycles the spiral at about 98% known words.
- Readings are the natural place where old items meet new contexts.

## 11. Roots and patterns: Hebrew's built-in "old information"

**11.1 Improve: teach root (שֹׁרֶשׁ) and pattern (מִשְׁקָל / בִּנְיָן) awareness explicitly and gradually. (Grade C/D)**

Advanced L2 readers don't extract roots the way natives do. Yet roots and patterns are the best possible anchors for connecting new words to known ones.

> *Illustrative, proposed — needs Guy's review:* once **כָּתַב** and **מִכְתָּב** are established, **כְּתוֹבֶת** and **כַּתָּב** can be introduced as "same root כ־ת־ב, new pattern", with a guess-first prompt.

**11.2 Improve: introduce related words from one root across lessons, not all at once. (Grade B)**

- Same-root words can compete with each other, just like semantic sets (§1).
- Spread them out and anchor each one to an established family member.

## 12. Vocabulary specifics

What the evidence says:

- **Repetition matters; spacing and engagement moderate it** (r = .34 for incidental learning; Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa, 2019). **Grade A.**
- **Deliberate flashcard and word-list study gives large gains; writing and fill-in-the-blank give small ones. Delayed retention falls sharply** (about 39% for meaning recall and 25% for form recall), so review is essential (Webb et al., 2020). **Grade A.**
- **"Evaluation" (choosing which word fits a context) contributes most to retention, then "need"; "search" adds nothing** (Yanagisawa & Webb, 2021). **Grade A/B.**
- **Productive retrieval (L1→L2) builds both receptive and productive knowledge** (Nakata). **Grade B.**
- **Pictures can help vocabulary, but learners overrate how much** (Carpenter & Olson, 2012). **Grade C.**

**12.1 Preserve: a dedicated Vocabulary step with retrieval practice. (Grade A)**

**12.2 Improve: favour "which word fits this sentence?" over "type the word". (Grade A/B)**

- Choosing a word for a sentence context is the "evaluation" component, which contributes most to retention.
- It fits sentence-centred design.

**12.3 Improve: include L1→L2 retrieval for core words, after recognition is secure. (Grade B)**

**12.4 Reconsider: pictures. (Grade C)**

- Useful for concrete nouns.
- They create overconfidence unless followed by retrieval.
- They also cost authoring time.

## 13. Motivation, metacognition and staying with the course

What the evidence says:

- **App-based beginner courses can reach real outcomes.** Duolingo beginner courses reached Intermediate Low reading (comparable to four university semesters) and Novice High listening; listening lagged (Jiang et al., 2021). **Grade B.**
- **Dropout is the dominant risk.** About 43% dropped out within three months in a large adult study. Learners with high acceptance of the app had 45–47% lower dropout risk, and breaks were not fatal (Hwang et al., 2024). **Grade B.**
- **Autonomy, competence and relatedness are linked to satisfaction and persistence** (self-determination theory). The evidence is mostly correlational. **Grade C.**
- **Learners prefer what feels easy, not what works** (desirable difficulties; Bjork). **Grade A.**
- Industry claims about streaks and gamification are low-quality evidence. **Grade D.**

**13.1 Improve: make competence visible through delayed success. (Grade C/D)**

- Example: "You remembered 18 of 20 words from last week."
- Not through counts of completed screens.

**13.2 Improve: briefly explain the method to learners. (Grade A/D)**

- "Mistakes during review are part of how this works."
- This counters the fluency illusion so learners don't skip spaced retrieval.

**13.3 Improve: make returning after a break easy. (Grade B/D)**

- Offer a short "welcome back" review instead of a penalty or a lost streak.

**13.4 Reconsider: gamification. (Grade D)**

- Light, honest progress signals are reasonable.
- Heavy streak mechanics have weak independent evidence.

## 14. Making it manageable to author

Course quality depends on Guy being able to produce lessons sustainably. These are content-design principles, not build instructions.

**14.1 Preserve: a fixed lesson recipe. (Grade D)**

- The same five parts every time cuts authoring decisions and learner load alike.

**14.2 Improve: author each sentence once, with its target and its known parts noted. (Grade D)**

- Record for each sentence its one target and the established items it reuses.
- Then the spiral, "one new thing per sentence", and translation targets can all be checked rather than remembered.

**14.3 Improve: put authoring effort where the research says it pays most. (Grade D)**

In order:

1. good example sentences
2. native audio
3. targeted error feedback
4. scheduling the reuse of old items

Low priority:

- long explanations
- decorative media
- large numbers of exercise types

**14.4 Preserve: human-made content stays canonical. (project rule)**

- Generated or synthetic material is always labelled.
- It never replaces Guy's teaching or linguistic judgment without approval.

## 15. Proposed per-lesson design checklist

This extends the per-lesson template in Guy's Paul Nation summary. *Proposed — for Guy's review.*

1. **Anchor:** which established items does this lesson connect to? Is each anchor retrieved before the new item is shown?
2. **Target:** what is the one new structure, plus the new words? Is any look-alike being introduced at the same time? If so, split it.
3. **Learn:** one idea, two or three example sentences with audio, an explicit contrast with the anchor or with English.
4. **Check:** can the learner only succeed by attending to the target form?
5. **Vocabulary:** recognition → recall → production, both directions, sentence context where possible.
6. **Apply:** one target per sentence, the rest established material, targeted feedback for predictable errors, translation items each with an explicit target.
7. **Fluency:** at least one fast, easy activity on known material.
8. **Continue / spiral:** which earlier items are retrieved today? Which of today's items are scheduled to return in the next lessons, about a week later, and in a later unit?
9. **Nikkud and captions:** what stage of fading applies?
10. **Provenance:** is every item human-verified, proposed, or synthetic, and labelled as such?

## 16. Open decisions for Guy

1. **Learner profile:** English-speaking adults only? Is the alphabet already known when the course starts?
2. **Spiral split:** how much reuse is authored into lessons vs left to a personal review scheduler?
3. **Nikkud fade:** should there be a fade, and on what trigger: mastered patterns or course position?
4. **Voices:** is recording with several native speakers feasible?
5. **Speaking:** how much, and whether to use recording or speech-recognition feedback.
6. **Grammar scope:** the Bet/Gimel checklist items in §5.4.
7. **Translation share:** keep the roughly 20–30% guideline?
8. **Guess-first prompts:** allow root-based guessing before new items are revealed?
9. **Fluency strand:** accept timed or speeded practice on known material?
